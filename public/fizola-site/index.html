Behind the ad.

Fizola isn’t a real cola. The brand, the can, the street, the song and the commercial were all created and directed from scratch, with no cameras and no studio. Here is how it was made.

01 · The brand

Built to feel remembered.

The brand needed to feel familiar before the audience knew anything about it.

Fizola Cola. 355 mL can. Slogan: Turn on the sunshine.

The logo was built as packaging, not as a technology mark. “Fizola” in a heavy retro script, tilted slightly upward, with stacked offset stripes in harvest gold, burnt orange and cola brown behind the letters. A rounded pill underneath reads COLA.

It comes in two versions. Cream for the can and dark backgrounds. Tomato red for light ones.

The can

Glossy tomato red. The cream logo at the centre. Three bands of gold, orange and brown around the lower third. A silver top. It needed to read instantly, on a step, in a wave, in someone’s hand, and on the end card.

The palette

Five colours, all pulled from the decade. Warm, saturated and a little sun-faded, like a magazine ad that has been on a fridge door since 1975.

02 · The world

Lock the world before you shoot it.

A commercial only works if you believe the world it creates. So I built the world first, then locked it.

Juno

Juno is the face of the spot. Early twenties, warm brown skin, freckles, big natural curls in a high puff with a burnt-orange scrunchie, and gold hoops. Her outfit is pure 1970s: a harvest-gold ribbed collared top tucked into high-waisted, light-wash flared jeans, with white high-tops. Two friends join her, dressed for the same decade.

Maple Street

The location is a fictional residential street called Maple Street. Brick townhouses in mint, peach and butter yellow. Front stoops with potted geraniums. A corner store with a red-and-white striped awning. A red fire hydrant.

Every detail supports the same feeling: a hot, slightly boring summer afternoon that is about to become something else.

The consistency system

Generative video becomes difficult when the world keeps changing underneath the story. So I used the consistency system I use on every AI production. Four master references: the can, Juno’s portrait, Juno full body, and Maple Street.

Each reference received one canonical description, repeated word for word across the prompts. Each shot had an approved keyframe before motion was generated. The logo was attached as an image ingredient so the model reproduced the actual lettering instead of inventing a new version. Every can in the film inherits that same master reference.

03 · The idea

Colour is the story.

The commercial begins on a sleepy, washed-out summer afternoon. Juno sits on her stoop in the heat. Then the camera finds a cold can of Fizola on the step.

The music drops. A wave of cola and ice sweeps through the frame, and Maple Street comes back in full colour.

The colour is the narrative. The drained world is the afternoon before the product: flat, hot and bored. The can brings the colour back.

Turn on the sunshine, and the whole street lights up with you. That is why the slogan is the plot of the commercial as well as its last line.

The visual reference is specific: a 1970s summer afternoon remembered fondly, rather than a parody of the era. Heat. Boredom. A cold can. Then everything comes alive. Nostalgia as a feeling, not a costume.

04 · The nine shots

One idea per shot.

Thirty seconds. Nine shots. Every shot has one job.

Shot 01 · 0:00 · The lull

Start quiet.

Juno sits on her stoop in the heat. A slow push in. The colour was drained further in the edit.

The world needed to feel flat before the colour arrived, so the change would feel earned.

Shot 02 · 0:05 · Cold can

Let the move breathe.

One continuous push in, from the side of the can to the full lid, with cold vapour curling off the top.

An early cut trimmed it short. The move is the build, so the final cut keeps all of it, right up to the drop.

Shot 03 · 0:09 · Cola wave

The transition is the product.

The drop lands and a wave of cola and ice sweeps through the frame. The drink itself becomes the transition.

In the first part of the generated clip, the logo warped as the wave passed over it. The edit starts after that moment.

Shot 04 · 0:11 · Maple Street

Keep it simple.

Maple Street in full colour. Juno and two friends, each holding a can, laughing together. A hydrant sprays in the background.

The first version had a crowd clapping behind them, and it looked wrong. Cropping would have cut into the main three, so the shot was regenerated with only Juno and her friends. The logo sits as a small badge in the corner, clear of every face.

Shot 05 · 0:16 · The pour

The studio moment.

A hand tilts the can. A can pouring on its own can feel artificial. A hand gives the product a physical relationship with the world.

Slowed to 60 percent. The shot starts as an ice cube falls into the glass. Earlier in the clip, a cube disappeared into the table, so that part never made the cut.

Shot 06 · 0:19 · Bubble rush

Pure sensation.

The camera rides the bubbles upward through the ice and into the light.

Two seconds of pure appetite.

Shot 07 · 0:21 · The laugh

The payoff.

Juno laughs, can in hand, confetti drifting around her.

Early in the clip her hair changed from a high puff to loose curls on screen. The shot starts after the change, so viewers only ever see one Juno.

Shot 08 · 0:24 · Hero float

The beauty shot.

The can hangs still at the centre of the frame. Ice, bubbles and cola droplets drift around it on a gold-to-orange gradient.

For a moment in the generated clip, the label doubled. The edit uses only the clean section after it. The product holds still. Everything else moves.

Shot 09 · 0:26 · Packshot

Land the line.

One can, one glass, nothing behind them. The camera pushes in as the can drifts left.

The end card sits in a narrow column on the left so it never touches the product. On the final hit of the song, the logo and the slogan pulse together.

Cut · The vapour burst

Cut what isn’t needed.

A side-on macro of the opened can with a burst of cold vapour. It was in the first two cuts of the film.

Then it went. The cold-can push in already did its job, and the film was stronger without the repeat. A good shot still has to earn its place.

05 · The song

A cola needs a song.

The music is an original song called “Turn On The Sunshine”, written for the brand and produced in Suno. The hook is one word: Fizola.

The direction is 1970s funk, soul and disco at 112 BPM.

  • Slap bass
  • Wah guitar
  • Clavinet
  • Horn stabs
  • Cowbell and congas
  • Disco strings

Fi-zo-la, Fizola! Ice-cold sunshine cola. Turn it up for the summer crowd. Fizola… turn on the sunshine!

The song opens before it starts. For the first five seconds, while the street is still faded, you hear the hook muffled and far away, as if it is playing down the block. Then the song comes in clearly, and the band enters exactly on the drop as the cola wave hits.

The full song runs 55 seconds. The commercial runs 30. The edit uses the opening through the first chorus, then joins to the song’s ending. The verse didn’t survive the cut, so its information moved into the graphics:

Ice cold!Refreshing!Super chill

And a sticker: A party in every sip. What the ear doesn’t get, the eye gets.

06 · The edit

Cut on the beat. Keep it honest.

Three rules governed the edit: cut on the beat. Trim every clip to its strongest seconds. Keep the edit honest. No effect should contradict what is happening on screen.

There were four versions over two days.

The first cut had a can-crack sound dropped into a muted beat before the drop. It sounded like the song had been cut off. The crack went, and the song now plays unbroken.

The on-screen words started as pills. They became retro lettering with stacked gold, orange, red and brown shadows, and each letter bounces in on its own.

A tenth shot, a burst of vapour off the opened can, was cut entirely in the third version.

The ending needed more weight. A final hit was built on the last downbeat, and the logo and slogan pulse with it.

The last pass was all sound. The muffled opening was raised so it reads as music, and the vocals were brought forward so the lyrics land.

07 · The pipeline

The same machine. A different film.

Every stage was directed by a person and executed with AI-assisted tools. The technology expanded what one director could produce in a session. It did not make the creative decisions.

StageTool
Shot planning, lyrics, prompts, assemblyClaude (Anthropic)
Reference imagery and keyframesGoogle Flow
MotionGemini Omni 1.1 Flash
SongSuno
Compositing, graphics, mix, renderHyperFrames (open source)

There were no cameras. No studio. No traditional editing timeline. Nine generated clips, each trimmed to its strongest seconds. Every graphic drawn as an overlay. Every creative decision made before generation or in the edit.

When something didn’t work, the answer was never to accept it because AI made it. Cut it. Change it. Re-render it. That is the real point of the pipeline. AI didn’t replace the director. It changed what one director could make.
