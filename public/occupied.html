00 · The idea

Make the weakness the subject.

The challenge. The best-known failure of AI video is the face. Keep one character the same across twenty shots and the tools start to drift. Most AI films hide that problem. OCCUPIED is built on it: a film about one face that keeps turning up where it should not be.

The constraints. Ninety seconds. No exposition. Short dialogue that never depends on a perfect lip sync. One clear idea, escalated three times, with an ending that answers the opening shot for shot.

My role. Story, screenplay, look, cast and location design, shot design, voice direction, score direction and the edit. Directed end to end.

The point. AI is a crew with strengths and weaknesses, like any crew. A director’s job is to write for the crew you have. Here the limits of the tools shaped the story, and the story is better for it.

01 · The story

The opening and the ending rhyme.

A cold open that the last act repeats in daylight. A dream timestamp that becomes a real one. Doubles who are only ever seen from behind, until they are not. And a last frame that hands the question to the audience.

03 · The look

A feature film, not an ad.

Real skin, motivated light, lens character and restraint in the camera. Every prompt was written to the standard of a feature film, not a commercial.

  • Anamorphic. Oval bokeh and gentle horizontal flares, from practical lights only.
  • Scope. Finished at 2.39:1. Every shot was framed with heads and action in the middle band, so the letterbox never cuts a face.
  • Slow camera. Dolly, push-in, lateral track. Handheld only once Eli panics.
  • Symmetry. A one-point corridor of doors for the climax, in the tradition of classic horror framing.

The grade

Cool teal shadows, muted skin and print-film contrast. One unified grade, grain and halation sit over every clip in the edit, so shots from different generations read as one film. Eli’s rust overshirt is the only saturated warm colour in the film. Once the audience clocks it in the crowd, every rust-orange shape becomes a threat.

04 · Writing for the crew

Direct around what the tools do badly.

Two people with the same face in one frame is the hardest shot to generate. This film never asks for it.

Lock the face first

Three reference images lock Eli, including one of him from behind. One canonical description of him is pasted word for word into every prompt and never paraphrased. The doubles are built from the same three references, because they are the same person.

Locations follow the same rule. Each place gets a reference image, and once it exists the shot prompts point to the picture instead of describing the room again. The picture, not the words, sets the room.

Stage the shot for the model

  • One face per frame. Whenever Eli and a double share a shot, one of them is seen from behind.
  • Let the camera move. The model is strong on light, rooms and camera moves, weaker on hands and bodies. Bodies stay still; the camera travels.
  • Approve the still first. Every shot starts as a keyframe, signed off as a still, then animated from that frame.
  • Small mouths. Speaking faces are kept small, in profile or off screen.

05 · The voices

The doubles speak in Eli’s voice.

AI video does not give a reliable lip sync or a consistent voice from shot to shot. So the film was written so it never needs one. Every line is recorded with one locked voice per character and placed by hand in the edit, and most lines play where no mouth has to match: through glass, over hands, over a listener, over a back.

The doubles use the same voice as Eli. That sameness is the scare. In the mix they sit drier and closer, as if they are slightly too near the microphone. “You’re early” is heard over Eli’s frozen face, never seen on the double’s mouth.

06 · Score and sound

The street goes quiet before the doors do.

One original instrumental carries the film, cut to picture. A drone under the dream, then silence on the title card until an espresso machine pulls us into the cafe. A piano motif sits low under the dialogue. On the walk, a ticking pulse and rising strings take over while the street sound slowly thins out. The doors bring the full dissonant climax, the pounding cut to the beat. Then a hard cut to silence, and only the phone.

The shape of the score

The planned shape of the score, not measured levels.

Sound effects carry the realism

Rain on the cafe window, the espresso machine, fists on doors, the phone buzz. The clips’ own ambience is kept where it is clean and rebuilt where it is not. Breaths and gasps come from a sound library, never from a voice tool.

Mixed for the screen it plays on

Dialogue held around −24 LUFS short-term, the final mix around −16 LUFS integrated for online play. Nothing is ever laid over a face or a double.

The story began as my skeleton: a dream, a walk home, a door. Development turned it into a structure that pays off, a screenplay of twelve lines and a shot plan where every shot has an approved keyframe before anything moves.

Overlays are deliberately few: a dream timestamp, the title card, and a phone notification on black. Everything else is picture and sound.

The tools generated the material. Every decision about what stayed, what moved and what got cut was made by a director.
