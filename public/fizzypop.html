02 · The backstory

The opposite test.

ORIVEX ARC-7 was a technical product film. Precision, narration, a sterile lab, a robotic arm. It proved the pipeline could direct something exact.

ORIVEX ARC-7 · the first test →

Fizzy Pop is the opposite test.

A soda commercial. Character, comedy, color, music and nostalgia.

Same AI-assisted production pipeline. Completely different creative problem.

The question was simple: can an AI-assisted production create a 1970s-style TV spot that feels like a Saturday morning memory?

Not a tech demo. Not a showcase of AI tricks.

A real commercial, with a brand you could believe had always existed and a jingle you might find yourself humming afterward.

So I invented a cola, gave it a summer, and directed the entire spot without a camera.

03 · The brand

Built to feel remembered.

The brand needed to feel familiar before the audience knew anything about it.

Fizzy Pop Cherry Cola. 355 mL can. Tagline: Open loud.

I designed the logo as packaging, not as a technology mark. Heavy rounded italic lettering, stacked and tilted slightly upward. A starburst breaks out from behind the final letter, with three bubbles rising from it. A sunflower-yellow pill underneath reads CHERRY COLA.

It needed to work instantly. On a can. Across a room. In motion. At the end of a commercial.

The palette

The color system became part of the personality. Bright and cheeky, but with enough gloss and restraint to feel like a real consumer brand rather than a cartoon.

04 · The world

Lock the world before you shoot it.

A commercial only works if you believe the world it creates. So I built the world first, then locked it.

Juno

Juno is the face of the spot. Early twenties, warm brown skin, freckles, big natural curls in a high puff with a cherry-red scrunchie, gold hoops, a sunflower-yellow tee and cherry-red shorts. Two friends complete the block party.

Maple Street

The location is a fictional residential street called Maple Street. Brick townhouses in mint, peach and butter yellow. Front stoops with potted geraniums. A corner store with a red-and-white striped awning. A red fire hydrant.

Every detail exists to support the same feeling: a hot, slightly boring summer afternoon that is about to become something else.

The consistency system

Generative video becomes difficult when the world keeps changing underneath the story. So I used the same consistency system developed for ORIVEX. Four master references: the can, Juno’s portrait, Juno full body, Maple Street.

Each reference received one canonical description, repeated word for word across the prompts. Each shot had an approved keyframe before motion was generated. The can’s logo was attached as an image ingredient so the model reproduced the actual lettering instead of inventing a new version. Every can in the film inherits that same master reference.

05 · The idea

Color is the story.

The commercial begins with a sleepy, washed-out summer afternoon. One thing is different. The can is in color.

Juno cracks it open. The music drops. A wave of cherry cola sweeps through the frame, and the street comes alive in full color as a block party.

The color isn’t decoration. It is the narrative. The drained world represents the afternoon before the product: flat, hot and bored. The pop restores it.

Open loud, and the world gets loud with you. That is why the tagline isn’t simply a line at the end of the commercial. It is the plot.

The visual reference is specific: a 1970s summer afternoon remembered fondly, rather than a parody of the era. Heat. Boredom. A cold can opening. Then everything comes alive. Nostalgia as a feeling, not a costume.

06 · The ten shots

One idea per shot.

Thirty seconds. Ten shots. Roughly three seconds each. Every shot has one job.

Shot 01 · 0:00 · The lull

Start quiet.

Juno sits on her stoop. Heat haze. A slow push in. The color was drained further in the edit.

The world needed to feel flat before the color arrived, so the eventual transformation would feel earned.

Shot 02 · 0:05 · Cold can

The product is the color.

The can is the only saturated object in the frame. That’s the entire idea.

Cold mist curls from the top while the street remains gray behind it.

Shot 03 · 0:07 · The pop

The signature moment.

Hands are one of the harder things for generative models to get right. So this shot was constrained from both ends. A start frame. An end frame. The model only had to generate the small movement between them: one finger lifting the tab into place.

The can crack itself is real recorded audio. Nothing sprays out. The restraint makes the moment feel more premium.

Shot 04 · 0:09 · Splash wipe

The transition is the product.

A wave of cola, ice and cherries fills the screen. We cut to the party on the exact frame where it covers everything.

A dissolve would simply transition between two images. The wipe does something better. The drink itself becomes the transition.

Shot 05 · 0:11 · Block party

Move gently.

Maple Street returns in full color. Juno and her friends sway as the party comes alive. Nobody spins. Nobody performs exaggerated choreography.

Fast body movement is one of the places generative video can lose its shape, so the energy comes from the camera arc, confetti and editing rather than frantic movement. The logo sits as a small badge in the corner. Never over a face.

Shot 06 · 0:15 · The pour

The studio moment.

A hand tilts the can. That small decision matters. A can floating on its own can feel artificial. A hand gives the product a physical relationship with the world.

Slow-motion pour. Foam to the rim. A cherry drops into the glass with a crown splash.

Shot 07 · 0:18 · Bubble rush

Pure sensation.

The camera rides the bubbles upward through the ice. Then breaks through the surface into the light.

Two seconds of pure appetite.

Shot 08 · 0:20 · The sip

The payoff.

Juno lowers the can. Her eyes open wide. She laughs, confetti caught in her curls.

The expression had to feel spontaneous rather than generated. It took direction to get there.

Shot 09 · 0:23 · Hero float

The beauty shot.

The can hangs completely still at the center of the frame. Cherries, ice and fizz drift around it. A few elements fall softly into the foreground to create depth.

The product doesn’t move. Everything else does.

Shot 10 · 0:25 · Packshot

Use what is good.

The end card sits in the clear upper left. The first half of the generated clip contained a ghost glass collapsing in the background. So I didn’t try to fix it.

I used the clean second half, slowed it down to fill the shot, and cut the problem out. Cut what isn’t good. Keep what is.

07 · The song

A soda commercial needs a jingle.

The original plan was a voiceover over an instrumental track. I changed the plan. The commercial needed a jingle.

The hook is three words: Fizzy Pop.

The musical direction combines a 1970s television jingle with modern funk-pop at 116 BPM.

  • Bright female lead
  • Gang vocals on the hook
  • Handclaps
  • Slap bass
  • Brass stabs
  • Wah guitar
  • Glockenspiel
  • Whistle

The structure of the song became part of the directing. It opens sleepy and half-time, matching the faded street. Then there is one beat of silence. The can crack lands at 9.25 seconds. The musical drop arrives at 9.61. The sound moment lives inside the gap rather than being layered over the drop. It’s an old advertising trick. It still works.

Fizzy Pop, Fizzy Pop! Cherry cola, crack the top. Turn it up for the summer crowd. Fizzy Pop… open loud!

The complete song runs 68 seconds. The commercial runs 30. The edit uses the intro, the first chorus, then jumps to the horn-stab button ending. The verse and pre-chorus didn’t survive the cut. So their information moved into the graphics:

REAL CHERRY · WILD FIZZ · SUPER CHILL
And a sticker: every sip, a little party

Nothing sung is wasted. What the ear doesn’t get, the eye gets.

08 · The edit

Cut on the beat. Keep it honest.

Three rules governed the edit: cut on the beat. Trim every clip to its strongest seconds. Keep the edit honest. No effect should contradict what is actually happening on screen.

There were three major passes. The first version opened the color world with a white flash. The flash went. The final transition cuts directly from the can crack into the cola wave.

The logo originally appeared as a large centered slam over the party. It looked impressive for about two seconds. Then it became obvious it was covering people’s faces. So it became a small badge in the corner.

The graphic pills were reduced in size and moved onto clean yellow. The packshot’s ghost glass was removed entirely.

Then came a second finish: the same film with flat, shadow-free graphics.

Runtime30 seconds exactly
Picture1920 × 1080
Frame rate24 fps
MixAround −18.5 LUFS

The jingle runs at full level throughout, with the real can crack tucked into its silent musical beat.

Two finishes. The final film exists in two versions: on-screen graphics with drop shadows, and the same cut and mix with shadow-free graphics. Same film. Different finish.

09 · The pipeline

The same machine. A different film.

Every stage was directed by a person and executed with AI-assisted tools. The technology expanded what one director could produce in a session. It did not make the creative decisions.

StageTool
Direction, script, lyrics, prompts, assemblyClaude (Anthropic)
Reference imagery and keyframesGoogle Flow
MotionGemini Omni 1.1 Flash
JingleSuno
Compositing, graphics, mix, renderHyperFrames (open source)

There were no cameras. No studio. No traditional editing timeline. Ten generated clips, each trimmed to its strongest seconds. Every graphic drawn as a vector overlay. Every creative decision made before generation or in the edit.

And when something didn’t work, the answer wasn’t to accept it because AI made it. Cut it. Change it. Re-render it. That’s the real point of the pipeline. AI didn’t replace the director. It changed what one director could make.
