Fizzy Pop + Fizola

One brief. Three films.

Two fictional cola brands. Two 30-second commercials. One production system. I made Fizzy Pop and Fizola from the same creative foundation, then deliberately pushed them in different directions.

The exercise was simple: how much can change when the underlying production system stays the same?

The answer is quite a lot.

The world, character, references, duration and AI-assisted pipeline stayed consistent. The personality, music, pacing, colour treatment, compositions and editorial decisions changed.

That made the two films a useful creative experiment: not just two ads, but two different answers to the same brief.

02 · The brands

Designed to feel like they were remembered, not invented yesterday.

Both brands were designed as packaging first. Not technology brands. Not AI brands. Not fake startup identities. Colas.

The goal was to make each one feel as though you could have encountered it on a supermarket shelf in another decade.

Neither logo is derived from a real cola brand. They simply approach the same era from different directions. Fizzy Pop is brighter, cheekier and more energetic. Fizola is warmer, softer and slightly sun-faded.

Fizzy Pop Cherry Cola

Heavy rounded italic lettering, stacked and tilted upward, with a starburst and three rising bubbles. A sunflower-coloured pill carries the words CHERRY COLA.

Bright. Loud. A little ridiculous. Exactly right.

03 · The songs

Two hooks for one summer.

The commercials didn’t just need background music. They needed brands that sounded like they had existed before.

Fizzy Pop!

Jingle · 116 BPM · 68 seconds

The first idea was a voiceover over an instrumental. It became a jingle instead.

The musical direction was a 1970s television commercial crossed with modern funk-pop: bright female lead, gang vocals, handclaps, slap bass, brass stabs, wah guitar, glockenspiel and whistle.

Fizzy Pop, Fizzy Pop! Cherry cola, crack the top. Turn it up for the summer crowd. Fizzy Pop… open loud!

The key production decision was silence. The intro starts sleepy. Then everything drops out for one beat. A real can crack lands at 9.25 seconds. The musical drop follows at 9.61.

The sound isn’t layered over the music. The music makes room for it.

Download the jingle (MP3)

Fizola

Turn On The Sunshine · 112 BPM · 90 seconds

Fizola became a full original song rather than a traditional jingle.

The reference point was 1970s funk, soul and disco: slap bass, wah guitar, clavinet, horn stabs, cowbell, congas and disco strings.

Fi-zo-la, Fizola! Ice-cold sunshine cola. Turn it up for the summer crowd. Fizola… turn on the sunshine!

The song starts before it starts. For five seconds, the hook is muffled and distant, as though it’s playing somewhere down the block. Then the band enters with the wave.

Download the song (MP3)

Writing for the edit

Neither commercial has room for a full song. So neither song was written only for the final 30 seconds.

The full tracks give the brands a larger identity. The commercials then take the strongest pieces and build around them.

When the verse gets cut, its information doesn’t disappear. It moves into the graphics.

What the ear doesn’t get, the eye gets.

04 · What stayed the same

The world stayed locked. The creative choices didn’t.

Both films live on Maple Street, a fictional neighbourhood built around the same visual language.

Maple StreetBrick townhouses in mint, peach and butter yellow. A striped awning. A red hydrant. A slightly faded summer afternoon.
JunoThe same character, with the same face, curls and freckles, restyled for each brand.
Four master referencesThe can, Juno’s portrait, Juno full body and the street. Each had a fixed description and served as a visual anchor throughout production.
Colour is the storyBoth films begin in a muted world. The cola becomes the visual trigger that brings the colour back.
The format30 seconds exactly. 1920 × 1080. 24 fps. The musical drop lands at roughly nine seconds.
The pipelineClaude, Google Flow, Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash, Suno and HyperFrames, in that order.

The tools stayed largely the same.

The decisions didn’t.

Thirty seconds, shot by shot

Each block represents one shot. Fizola spends more time on fewer images. The cold-can push, for example, runs four seconds instead of two. The separate tab-pull shot disappears completely. That sounds like a small editorial decision. It changes the feel of the entire commercial.

How much of each song makes the final cut?

Both songs were written as complete pieces, but the commercials use only their strongest 30 seconds. The edits keep the opening and first chorus, then jump forward to the ending. The verse doesn’t survive the cut. So the information that doesn’t fit in the music moves into the graphics instead. What the ear doesn’t get, the eye gets.

Fizzy Pop!FizolaSolid: used in the ad · Lighter: rest of the song

A note on tempo. The songs are only four BPM apart. They don’t sound four BPM apart. That’s because tempo isn’t doing all the work. Arrangement is.

Fizzy Pop opens sleepy and half-time before exploding into handclaps, gang vocals and glockenspiel. Fizola settles into a looser disco groove built around clavinet, cowbell, congas, strings and horns.

Same decade. Different energy.

07 · The pipeline

Same machine. Different decisions.

Every stage was directed by a person and executed with AI-assisted tools. The pipeline stayed consistent across both films. The creative direction did not.

StageTool
Shot planning, lyrics, prompts, assemblyClaude (Anthropic)
Reference imagery and keyframesGoogle Flow
MotionGemini Omni 1.1 Flash
MusicSuno
Compositing, graphics, mix, renderHyperFrames (open source)

There was no camera crew. No studio. No traditional editing timeline.

But that doesn’t mean there was no production process. Every generated clip was evaluated, trimmed and often regenerated.

  • A warped logo gets cut.
  • A disappearing ice cube gets cut.
  • A strange crowd gets removed.
  • A character’s hair changes halfway through a shot? Start later.
  • A shot almost works? Generate it again.

The working rule was simple: Cut it. Change it. Re-render it.

The technology generates possibilities. The director decides which ones survive.

The project

Fizzy Pop and Fizola are fictional brands created as production demonstrations. The brands, products, logos, music and commercials are original creations.

The project explores what happens when traditional creative direction is applied to an AI-assisted production workflow.

The interesting part isn’t that AI made the images. It’s what happens when someone treats those images like raw production material.

08 · The Japanese version

プシュッと あけよう。
Fizzy Pop goes Japanese.

A 30-second commercial is a structure, not a language. So I rebuilt Fizzy Pop as a Japanese TV-commercial jingle: same bones, completely different voice.

The Japanese version keeps the exact architecture of the English song. Sleepy half-time intro. One beat of silence. The full-band drop. The shout-back verse. The “little party” line. One horn stab to close.

Watch the Japanese version up top ↑

The song

Bright, funky J-pop summer cola jingle at about 117 BPM, sung entirely in Japanese. Punchy funk-pop groove with slap bass, choppy wah guitar, tight brass stabs, handclaps and a sparkly city-pop shine. Cheerful female lead vocal with very clear Japanese diction, plus a group shouting the call-and-response words back.

The brand hook lands on “フィジーポップ” (fee-jee-pop). And the slogan “プシュッと あけよう！” does real work: pushu is the Japanese sound of a can opening, so it functions as the Japanese version of “Open loud.”

Writing lyrics a machine can sing

The lyrics are written mostly in kana, the Japanese phonetic alphabet, rather than kanji. Suno pronounces every word correctly when it can sound each one out. Kanji readings are ambiguous. Kana is not.

Cutting the B version

The Japanese B version uses the same shots as the English cut, re-edited to the new song. A Japanese speech recognizer transcribed the vocal, and every on-screen word was timed to what she actually sings.

  • On-screen words switch to Japanese: “ほんものチェリー！”, “シュワシュワ！”, “キンキン！”, “ちょうチル”, each bouncing in on the beat.
  • The end-card slogan appears as it is sung: “プシュッと あけよう！”
  • The Fizzy Pop logo stays in English, the way it does on real Japanese packaging.
  • Product line and fine print are fully Japanese: “355mL缶・キンキンに冷やして” and “スペックCM。フィジーポップは架空のブランドです。”

One honest note: Suno didn’t render the one-beat silence before the drop in this take, so the drop lands straight after “あけよう”. The cut follows the song as delivered.

David Martin

Creative Director · North Vancouver, BC

I invented both colas, built their worlds, directed their songs and cut both commercials.

Making the same brief twice was the point. It forced me to separate the things that belonged to the production system from the things that belonged to the creative idea.
