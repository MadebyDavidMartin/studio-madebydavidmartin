## From tool user to director

### The old model

**Human → Tool → Output**

You ask. The model responds. You accept, reject, or try again. The human is essentially operating a very sophisticated interface.

### The director model

**Objective → Context → Crew → Production → Review → Approval**

The human establishes the system. AI becomes part of the crew. Different models and tools can perform different jobs. References become production infrastructure. Work moves through defined stages. Outputs are evaluated against a standard. The human remains responsible for the final decision.

**The shift is from prompting to directing.**

---

# 01 — Take the director's chair

Most people begin with the tool. They open ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Midjourney, Runway or another system and immediately start asking for things. The director starts somewhere else.

**What are we trying to make?**

The role comes before the prompt.

If you are directing an AI-generated video, you are responsible for the creative intent. If you are building an enterprise knowledge system, you are responsible for the information architecture. If you are generating a proposal, you are responsible for the argument. If you are deploying an AI agent, you are responsible for what the agent is allowed to do.

AI can contribute to the work. It cannot own the objective.

### The director's job

* Define the outcome.
* Establish the audience.
* Set the constraints.
* Define the quality bar.
* Provide the necessary context.
* Assign work to the appropriate tools or agents.
* Review the output.
* Make the final call.

**The first prompt is not the beginning of the production. The decision to direct is.**

## The Director's Stack

A useful AI production system has several layers.

### 01 — Direction

What are we trying to accomplish? The objective, audience, constraints and definition of success.

### 02 — Context

What does the system need to know? References, source material, brand standards, organizational knowledge, examples and constraints.

### 03 — Crew

Which tools, models, agents or people should do the work? Different systems have different strengths.

### 04 — Workflow

How does the work move? Research → draft → generate → evaluate → revise → approve.

### 05 — Memory

What needs to remain consistent across the production? Characters. Brand. Facts. Voice. Decisions. References.

### 06 — Quality control

How do we know the result is good enough? Standards, checklists, evaluation criteria and human review.

### 07 — Approval

Who decides what ships? The final gate remains human.

The tools will change. The stack will change. The architecture remains.

---

## Context is production

AI is only as useful as the context surrounding the task. A vague instruction produces a plausible answer. A well-designed context produces work that belongs in a specific organization, brand, production, or workflow.

That means context should not be treated as something you occasionally paste into a prompt. It should be designed.

A useful production context might include:

* Source documents
* Brand standards
* Previous work
* Character references
* Approved language
* Product information
* Audience definitions
* Constraints
* Examples of good work
* Examples of unacceptable work
* Decisions that have already been made

The result is a shift from: **"Tell the AI what to do."** to: **"Build the environment in which the AI can do the work correctly."**

That is context engineering. And in many AI systems, it is the difference between a demo and something you can actually use.

---

## The production loop

Every useful AI production can be thought of as a loop:

**01 — Define** — What are we trying to achieve?
**02 — Contextualize** — What does the system need to know?
**03 — Direct** — What should each person, model, tool or agent do?
**04 — Produce** — Generate the work.
**05 — Evaluate** — Does it meet the standard?
**06 — Refine** — What needs to change?
**07 — Approve** — Is this ready to ship?
**08 — Learn** — What should become part of the system for next time?

The final step matters. A good production does not simply create an output. It improves the system that creates the next output.

---

## Build a crew, not a single prompt

One of the most useful shifts in AI work is to stop asking: **Which AI tool should I use?** and start asking: **What crew does this production require?**

A production might need:

**The Researcher** — Finds information and establishes the facts.
**The Strategist** — Turns information into an approach.
**The Writer** — Creates the first draft.
**The Designer** — Translates the idea into a visual system.
**The Producer** — Moves the work through the workflow.
**The Critic** — Looks for weaknesses and inconsistencies.
**The Director** — Makes the decisions.

These roles can be performed by people, AI models, agents, software, or combinations of all three. The important thing is not the technology. It is the separation of responsibilities.

When every task goes to the same model with the same prompt, you have a tool. When different capabilities work together toward a defined outcome, you have a production system.

---

## The approval gate

Automation should remove repetitive work. It should not remove judgment.

Every production needs a point where someone asks: **Is this actually good enough?**

That question becomes more important, not less, as generation gets cheaper.

An approval gate might check:

* Accuracy
* Brand alignment
* Voice
* Visual consistency
* Strategic relevance
* Audience fit
* Compliance
* Completeness
* Factual grounding
* Production quality

The system can automate the checks. The director owns the decision.

**Nothing ships simply because a model said it was finished.**

---

## Not everything should be automated

The goal is not maximum automation. The goal is the right allocation of human and machine judgment.

AI is particularly good at:

* Generating variations
* Transforming information
* Summarizing
* Classifying
* Research assistance
* Pattern recognition
* First drafts
* Repetitive production
* Structured transformations
* Exploring possibilities

Humans remain particularly important for:

* Intent
* Taste
* Judgment
* Accountability
* Strategic decisions
* Sensitive communication
* Ambiguity
* Final approval

The strongest systems do not ask: **"How much can we automate?"** They ask: **"Where does human judgment create the most value?"**

# AI production maturity

### Level 01 — Experiment

You are trying AI tools. The workflow is mostly manual. **"Let's see what this can do."**

### Level 02 — Repeat

You have discovered useful prompts and workflows. The same tasks are becoming faster. **"We know how to get a decent result."**

### Level 03 — Systemize

Context, references, workflows and standards become reusable. **"We can produce this consistently."**

### Level 04 — Orchestrate

Multiple tools, agents and people work together. **"The system knows what happens next."**

### Level 05 — Direct

The human focuses increasingly on objectives, judgment and quality while the production system handles execution. **"We have built a production capability."**

The goal is not to reach Level 05 everywhere. The goal is to understand what level a particular problem actually requires.

# Proving ground 03

## Directing enterprise knowledge

The same principles that keep a creative production consistent can be applied to organizational knowledge.

The problem is familiar: Important information exists across documents, proposals, websites, presentations, emails and people's heads. AI can generate from that information. But generation is only useful if the underlying knowledge is trustworthy, structured and accessible. So the production system starts before the prompt.

I built an interconnected company knowledge system designed to give AI a reliable reference layer. The system established:

**A source of truth** — Information could be organized and referenced rather than rediscovered repeatedly.
**A shared context** — AI could work from the organization's actual language, services, capabilities and history.
**A repeatable workflow** — Knowledge could feed proposal, content and sales workflows rather than existing as a static archive.
**A human approval layer** — Generated work still passed through the people responsible for the result.

The result was not simply a better prompt. It was a better environment for AI to work in.

That is the larger principle: **Build the system around the intelligence, not just the interface to it.**

---

## Directing a proposal

A proposal is a perfect example of an AI production system. It contains research, requirements, organizational knowledge, strategy, writing, evidence, formatting, review and compliance.

The mistake is to ask AI to "write the proposal." The director breaks the production apart.

**Requirements → Knowledge → Strategy → Structure → Draft → Evidence → Review → Compliance → Approval**

AI can assist at almost every stage. But each stage needs a job, a source of truth and a quality standard.

The result is not AI replacing proposal expertise. It is AI increasing the amount of proposal production one person can direct.

The same principle can be applied to RFPs, bids, sales enablement, marketing campaigns and other complex knowledge workflows.

**The larger the production, the more important the system becomes.**

---

## From RFP to first draft

One of the clearest tests of an AI system is whether it changes the economics of real work. A complex RFP that previously required substantial manual reading, searching, organizing and drafting can now move through a structured AI-assisted workflow.

The objective is not: **"Let AI write the response."** It is: **Extract → Understand → Map → Research → Draft → Review**

The first draft can move from hours of manual work to roughly 30 minutes. That does not eliminate the proposal professional. It changes where their time goes. Less time finding and assembling information. More time thinking about the response. More time strengthening the argument. More time reviewing what actually matters.

**Automation creates the capacity. Direction determines the value.**

---

## Consistency is a systems problem

When one person creates one thing, consistency is easy. When ten people create fifty things, it becomes difficult. When AI can create five hundred things, consistency becomes an architectural problem.

The answer is not to inspect every output manually forever. It is to create systems that make the correct output easier to produce.

That means defining: The reference. The voice. The visual language. The rules. The examples. The workflow. The approval criteria.

Consistency should be designed into the production. **If the system has to remember the standard every time, the standard is not yet part of the system.**

---

## AI fluency is not knowing every AI tool.

The tools change too quickly. AI fluency is knowing how to think with them.

It means understanding:

**What should AI do?**
**What should humans do?**
**What context does the system need?**
**Which model or tool is appropriate?**
**How should the workflow be structured?**
**Where should automation stop?**
**How will the output be evaluated?**
**How does today's workflow become tomorrow's system?**

Someone who knows every button in an AI application can still produce poor work. Someone who understands the system can move between tools as the technology changes.

**The durable skill is direction.**

---

## This is not a prompting methodology.

Prompts matter. But prompts are only one layer of an AI production system.

A great prompt operating against poor context will still produce poor work. A good model working without a defined objective will still wander. An automated workflow without an approval gate can simply automate mistakes.

The bigger system is: **Direction + Context + Workflow + Tools + Evaluation + Judgment**

Prompting sits inside that system. It is not the system itself.

That distinction becomes increasingly important as AI moves from conversational assistants into agents, automated workflows and production environments.

# Before you build an AI workflow, ask five questions.

### 01 — What are we actually trying to accomplish?

Not what should the AI do. What should the organization, customer, audience or production achieve?

### 02 — What does the system need to know?

Identify the context before designing the prompt.

### 03 — What should humans do?

Protect judgment where judgment matters.

### 04 — What should the machines do?

Give AI the repetitive, generative and transformational work where it creates leverage.

### 05 — What is the approval gate?

Decide before production begins what must be true before the work ships.

Five questions can prevent an enormous amount of unnecessary AI complexity.

# The Director's Chair (creative-direction version)

Creative direction is the work of turning an idea into something people can see, feel and remember. The tools change. The responsibility doesn't.

A creative director decides what the work is trying to say, what it should look like, how it should feel, who it is for, and when it is good enough to ship.

AI changes how much of that work can be explored. It makes experimentation cheaper. It makes iteration faster. It makes entirely new forms of production possible. But it doesn't replace taste, judgment or direction. It gives the director a bigger crew.

**The Director's Chair is my approach to creative work in that new environment.**

Part creative direction. Part production thinking. Part technology. Always human judgment at the centre.

# The eight principles (creative-direction version)

01 — Start with the idea. Technology is not the idea. A model is not the idea. A visual style is not the idea. Start with what you are trying to make someone think, feel or do.

02 — Direct, don't decorate. Every visual decision should serve the idea. Good creative direction is not adding more. It is knowing what belongs and what doesn't.

03 — Give the work a world. Strong creative work has an internal logic. Colour, typography, composition, movement, sound, characters, environments and language should feel like they belong to the same world.

04 — Reference everything. AI makes references more powerful, not less. The right references communicate things words cannot.

05 — Separate exploration from execution. The first job is to discover what might be possible. The second is to make the strongest possibility real.

06 — Make the impossible testable. Creative technology is most interesting when it turns an idea that would have been too expensive, too slow or simply impossible into something you can actually prototype.

07 — Protect the human point of view. AI can generate infinite possibilities. Infinite possibilities are not a creative strategy. Someone still has to choose.

08 — Ship the work. Creative direction ultimately ends in a decision. Not another prompt. Not another variation. The work gets finished.

# Taste

AI can generate ten thousand options. Taste is knowing which one is worth pursuing.

Creative direction is partly the ability to recognize a strong idea before it is fully formed. It is knowing when something feels: Too obvious. Too polished. Too safe. Too familiar. Too much. Not enough. Or exactly right.

AI can help generate possibilities. It cannot outsource the responsibility of choosing among them.

**The more abundant creation becomes, the more valuable taste becomes.**

# Direction vs. execution

I am comfortable getting hands-on. I write. I storyboard. I generate. I edit. I build. I experiment. I learn new tools.

But hands-on production is not the same thing as production for its own sake. The reason I stay close to the work is so I understand what is actually possible. That lets me direct with more specificity.

I can move from: **"We should make something cinematic."** to: **"Let's separate the character identity pass from the camera motion, build the environment independently, and assemble the final sequence in post."**

The closer I get to the technology, the better I can direct the creative. **I don't need to do everything myself. I need to understand enough to know what is possible.**

# Creative leadership

Creative leadership is not about having the loudest idea in the room. It is about creating the conditions for good ideas to survive.

That means: Giving people a clear brief. Creating room for experimentation. Knowing when to challenge the work. Knowing when to get out of the way. Protecting the quality bar. Connecting creative decisions to business objectives. Translating between creative, technical and business teams. And making the decision when the room cannot agree.

The best creative teams do not need someone telling them what to do every minute. They need someone who can give them **direction worth following.**
